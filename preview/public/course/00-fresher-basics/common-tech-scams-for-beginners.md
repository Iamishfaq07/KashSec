# Common tech scams for beginners

Phishing (covered in depth in the next lesson) is one specific technique. This lesson is broader: the handful of tech-support and social scams that specifically target people newer to technology, described plainly so you can recognize them the moment they happen.

## The fake tech support call

A phone call, or a scary pop-up claiming "Your computer is infected — call this number immediately," leads to a scammer posing as tech support (often claiming to be from a well-known company like Microsoft or Apple). Their goal is either to get you to pay for fake "repairs," or to get remote access to your computer so they can steal information directly.

:::danger Real companies do not cold-call you about a virus
Microsoft, Apple, and legitimate tech companies do not proactively call or pop up a warning demanding you call a number to fix an infection. If you see this, close the browser window (using your task manager if it won't close normally) and never call the number shown. If someone calls claiming this, simply hang up.
:::

## The gift card scam

A caller or emailer claims to be from a government agency, a utility company, or even a relative in trouble — and asks you to pay a fee, fine, or bail using gift cards, reading the codes over the phone. This specific payment method is the giveaway: gift cards are essentially untraceable once the codes are shared, which is exactly why scammers prefer them.

:::tip A reliable rule of thumb
No legitimate government agency, utility company, or business will ever ask you to pay anything using gift cards. If you hear this request, it's a scam, full stop — regardless of how urgent or official the caller sounds.
:::

## The "grandparent" / family emergency scam

A message or call claims to be from a family member "in trouble" and urgently needing money, often over an unfamiliar messaging app or a slightly-off phone number, and pressuring you not to tell anyone else in the family. The urgency and secrecy are the two biggest warning signs together.

- **Verify independently** — call the family member directly using a phone number you already have saved, not one provided in the suspicious message.
- **Slow down on purpose** — genuine emergencies rarely require an irreversible decision (like a wire transfer or gift cards) within minutes; the pressure to act instantly is itself a manipulation tactic.

## Fake online marketplace listings

An item priced suspiciously below its real value, a seller who insists on payment outside the marketplace's normal system, or a buyer who "overpays" and asks for the difference back — all common patterns behind marketplace scams. If a deal feels engineered to rush you past your normal judgment, that's worth treating as a signal on its own.

## The one habit that defeats almost all of these

Every scam in this lesson depends on the same core trick: creating pressure to act *immediately*, before you have time to think it through or check with someone else. Slowing down and verifying independently — a callback to a known number, a second opinion from someone you trust — defeats nearly all of them, regardless of how convincing the specific story is.

## 🧪 Hands-on lab — build your own defense script

Write yourself a short, personal script for the next time you get a suspicious call or message: what will you say, and what will you check, before doing anything the caller or message asks? Keep it somewhere you'll actually see it again — a notes app, or written down near your phone.

## Key terms

- **Tech support scam** — a fake warning or call claiming your device is infected, aimed at extracting payment or remote access.
- **Social engineering** — manipulating someone into an action or disclosure through psychological pressure rather than a technical exploit (covered in much more depth in Path 10).

## Recap

- Legitimate companies don't cold-call about infections or demand you call a number from a scary pop-up.
- A request for gift-card payment from any "official" source is a reliable scam indicator on its own.
- Urgency and pressure to act immediately, especially combined with secrecy, are the common thread across nearly every scam in this lesson.
- Verifying independently — a callback to a known number, a second opinion — defeats almost all of them.
