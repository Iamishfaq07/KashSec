# Career Growth After Year One

The first-90-days lesson closed with owning and proposing. This final lesson covers what comes after that initial ramp-up period settles into steady, ongoing contribution — how career growth actually works once you're no longer the new hire, directly connecting to Path 16's specialise lesson's depth-over-breadth framing, now applied to the practical mechanics of actually getting there within a real organization.

## The plateau that catches people off guard

A genuinely common, rarely-discussed pattern: after the intense learning curve of the first 90 days (and often the following several months) settles down, many people hit a period where growth feels like it's slowed or stalled — the daily work has become genuinely familiar, but the next level of responsibility or skill isn't obviously visible or automatically granted. Recognizing this plateau as a normal, common phase (not a sign that growth has actually stopped, or that you've somehow failed) is the first step toward navigating through it deliberately rather than passively waiting for it to resolve itself.

## Making growth visible, not just real

Directly connecting to the first-90-days lesson's managing-up coverage: genuine skill growth that happens quietly, without ever being visible to the people who make promotion and opportunity decisions, often doesn't translate into actual career advancement — not because the growth wasn't real, but because visibility and advancement are structurally linked in most organizations. Concretely, this means proactively documenting and communicating specific accomplishments (not vague self-promotion, but concrete, specific examples: "reduced false-positive alert volume by 30% by tuning three detection rules") to your manager and, where appropriate, more broadly across the team.

## Choosing a specialization deliberately, with real information

Directly connecting to Path 16's specialise lesson: the first year or two in a role often provides genuine, first-hand exposure to multiple areas of security work (even in a single role, you likely touch adjacent domains occasionally) — this is valuable, real information for the specialization decision that lesson described, since choosing where to go deep based on actual, lived exposure to what the work is genuinely like day-to-day beats choosing based purely on how a specialization sounds from the outside, or from external prestige alone.

## Seeking stretch assignments deliberately

A genuinely effective, underused growth strategy: rather than waiting to be assigned more challenging work, proactively asking for a **stretch assignment** — a task slightly beyond your current comfort zone or formal scope, done with your manager's awareness and support — builds real capability faster than continuing to execute comfortably within an already-mastered scope. Directly connecting to the entry-level-vs-experienced-paths lesson's "specific, demonstrated impact" point: a stretch assignment, successfully completed, becomes exactly the kind of concrete accomplishment that supports both internal advancement and future external opportunities.

## When internal growth genuinely stalls

:::warning Recognize when the ceiling is organizational, not personal
Sometimes genuine, deliberate effort toward growth (visibility, stretch assignments, skill development) still doesn't translate into advancement within a specific organization — a limited number of senior roles, budget constraints, or organizational structure can create a genuine ceiling that has nothing to do with individual performance or growth. Directly connecting to the job-search and navigating-recruiters-and-referrals lessons: recognizing this pattern and beginning a deliberate, professional external job search at that point is a completely normal, common part of a healthy career trajectory, not a failure of loyalty or a sign that the growth effort was wasted — the skills and demonstrated impact built internally travel with you directly into that next opportunity.
:::

:::challenge Test yourself
Why can genuine, real skill growth still fail to translate into career advancement within an organization, and what does that imply about how growth should actually be pursued?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>Promotion and advancement decisions are typically made by people (a manager, a broader leadership team) who can only act on the information and evidence actually visible to them — quietly becoming more skilled at your job, without that growth ever being communicated through concrete, specific examples of impact, means the people making advancement decisions simply have no evidence to act on, regardless of how real and substantial the underlying growth genuinely was. This isn't a claim that visibility matters more than substance — the growth has to be genuinely real first, since visibility without substance behind it is easily seen through and damages credibility — but it does mean that pursuing growth effectively requires two connected habits working together: actually doing the work of becoming more skilled and capable, and deliberately, specifically communicating that growth through the managing-up habits the first-90-days lesson described, rather than assuming skilled work will automatically and inevitably be noticed and rewarded on its own. This is precisely why career growth, done well, is an active practice combining real capability development with intentional communication, not a passive process that happens automatically as a side effect of simply doing good work quietly.</details>
:::

## Key terms

- **Growth plateau** — the common, normal period after initial onboarding where growth feels stalled even though skill development continues.
- **Stretch assignment** — a task slightly beyond current scope, taken on deliberately and with manager support to build capability faster.
- **Organizational ceiling** — a genuine limit on internal advancement caused by structural factors, not individual performance.

## Recap

- The post-onboarding growth plateau is a normal, common phase, not a sign that growth has actually stopped.
- Genuine skill growth needs to be made visible through specific, concrete communication to actually translate into advancement.
- The first year or two provides real, lived information for the specialization decision, worth using deliberately.
- Stretch assignments build capability faster than staying within an already-mastered comfort zone, and recognizing an organizational ceiling when it exists is a normal part of a healthy career trajectory, not a personal failure.
