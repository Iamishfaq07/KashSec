Joining the security community

Security is one of the few technical fields where an unusually large amount of high-quality learning, mentorship, and even job opportunities flow through open, public community spaces rather than formal channels alone. This lesson is a short map of where that community actually lives.

:::

CTF competitions and platforms

Capture The Flag (CTF) competitions are gamified security challenges where you find hidden "flags" by exploiting deliberately vulnerable systems — Path 19's entire CTF Zone gives you dozens of practice challenges directly in this course, and platforms like TryHackMe, Hack The Box, and PicoCTF offer hundreds more, ranging from complete-beginner-friendly to genuinely difficult. Participating regularly is one of the single most effective ways to build real, practical skill outside of structured coursework.

Online communities worth knowing about

  • Reddit — communities like r/netsec and r/AskNetsec are active hubs for news, questions, and career discussion
  • Discord servers — many CTF platforms, security tool projects, and local meetup groups run active Discord communities where you can ask questions in real time
  • Twitter/X — a huge amount of cutting-edge security research and vulnerability disclosure discussion happens here first, among practitioners who post publicly
  • Local meetups and conferences — groups like DEF CON chapters (many cities have local "DC" groups) and OWASP local chapters run regular in-person meetups, often free, that are genuinely valuable for networking even as a complete beginner

Following real vulnerability research

Reading real, recently disclosed vulnerabilities — even ones far beyond your current skill level — builds intuition faster than almost anything else. CISA's advisories, security vendor blogs, and conference talk recordings (many major conferences post talks free on YouTube afterward) are all worth building into a casual reading habit, not something you need to fully understand yet, just something to keep in your peripheral vision as you learn.

Contributing back, even as a beginner

  • Answering questions you can already answer in a community you're part of is one of the fastest ways to solidify your own understanding — explaining something to someone else forces you to actually understand it yourself
  • Writing up your own CTF solves (Path 19's write-up template lesson covers exactly how) and sharing them publicly builds a visible portfolio while you're still learning, not just after you feel "ready"
  • Reporting bugs responsibly through official bug bounty programs, once you've built enough skill (Path 15 covers this in depth), is itself a form of community contribution with real financial upside

:::challenge Plan your first week You want to start engaging with the security community this week, but don't know where to begin.

HintWhat's a genuinely low-effort, low-pressure first step that doesn't require any specific skill level yet?
Show answerA strong first-week plan doesn't require any specific skill level: create an account on TryHackMe or Hack The Box and complete one beginner-friendly room or challenge; join one Discord or subreddit related to security and simply read discussions for a few days before posting; and follow two or three security researchers or CISA's advisory feed to start building the habit of casual exposure to real-world security news. None of this requires expertise — it requires showing up consistently, which is genuinely the hardest and most valuable part.

🧪 Hands-on lab — make your first move

  1. Create an account on TryHackMe or Hack The Box (both have free tiers) and complete one beginner room.

  2. Join one community space from this lesson (a subreddit, a Discord server) and simply observe for a few days.

  3. Bookmark CISA's advisories page or one security vendor blog, and check it once this week.

Key terms

  • CTF (Capture The Flag) — a gamified security challenge format where participants exploit deliberately vulnerable systems to find hidden flags.
  • DEF CON — one of the world's largest hacker conferences, with local chapter meetups in many cities.

Recap

  • Security has an unusually strong open community culture — CTFs, Discord servers, subreddits, and local meetups are all genuinely accessible starting points.
  • Regular CTF participation builds practical skill faster than reading alone.
  • Contributing back — answering questions, writing up solves, eventually reporting bugs responsibly — is valuable even while you're still learning.
  • None of this requires credentials or prior expertise to start participating in.
