Choosing your specialisation

Ethical hacking is a broad field. Most professionals eventually specialise. Based on what you've enjoyed most so far:

  • Loved Path 11 (web exploitation)? → Web application penetration testing, or bug bounty hunting (Path 15 is built exactly for this).
  • Loved Path 12 (network security)? → Network/infrastructure penetration testing, internal assessments, red teaming.
  • Curious about what happens after initial access? → Path 16's Active Directory and privilege escalation content is your natural next step.
  • Interested in cloud environments? → Path 16's cloud security content, plus specialised cloud security certifications.
  • Fascinated by AI systems? → Path 18 covers AI/LLM security — a rapidly growing, relatively less-crowded specialisation.
  • Enjoy the investigative, "what actually happened" side? → Path 16's digital forensics and incident response (DFIR) content.

There's no wrong choice — the methodology in this path applies across every one of these specialisations; only the specific technical toolkit changes.

Building real-world credibility

Beyond certifications (Path 14.1's OSCP/eJPT/CEH/CPENT), a few concrete ways to build a genuine, verifiable track record as you progress:

  • CTF (Capture The Flag) competitions — gamified security challenges (web, crypto, binary exploitation, forensics) that sharpen specific technical skills in a legal, structured environment. HackTheBox and TryHackMe offer excellent structured practice beyond CTFs too, with guided learning paths.
  • Bug bounty participation (Path 15) — real, disclosed findings on real platforms build a genuinely verifiable public track record.
  • Writing up your own labs and findings publicly — a technical blog documenting your learning (even your Metasploitable walkthroughs from this path) demonstrates communication skill directly, exactly the skill Path 14.7 emphasised.
  • Contributing to open-source security tools — even small contributions to tools like Nmap's NSE scripts, Nuclei templates, or Metasploit modules are genuinely noticed by hiring managers in this field.

A realistic first-year plan

This is a realistic, not overly optimistic, timeline for someone dedicating consistent part-time effort — and it matches the well-documented route from eJPT or CEH toward CPENT and OSCP over a similar multi-month arc.

Freelance and consultancy paths — the business side of the craft

Beyond employment at a security firm, a meaningful number of experienced testers eventually work independently — as freelance consultants or founders of small boutique testing firms. This path trades the steady structure of employment for direct control over client relationships, pricing, and specialisation focus, but it also demands a genuinely different skill set layered on top of the technical one: writing proposals and statements of work (a formalised, business-facing version of the scoping document from Path 14.2), managing client expectations and communication throughout an engagement, and handling the administrative and legal overhead of running a small business. It's not the right path for everyone, and most testers benefit from several years of employed experience first — but worth knowing it exists as a genuine long-term option, since the methodology in this path transfers completely regardless of who's signing your paycheck.

🧪 Lab — Your first end-to-end mini-engagement

Treat your Metasploitable VM as a genuine, if small, client engagement. Produce a complete mini-report covering the entire methodology from this path:

  1. A one-paragraph mock scope/RoE (Path 14.2) — pretend Metasploitable is "Example Corp's legacy internal server."
  2. A recon summary (Path 14.3) — even though it's a lab VM, note what you'd expect to find via OSINT in a real equivalent scenario.
  3. Your scanning/enumeration findings table (Path 14.4).
  4. Your exploitation log (Path 14.5).
  5. Post-exploitation notes — situational awareness commands run, any privesc paths identified (Path 14.6).
  6. At least one fully-written finding using the four-part structure (Path 14.7).
  7. A short executive summary tying it all together.

This complete document is genuinely portfolio-worthy — a real demonstration of methodology, not just isolated technique knowledge, and a strong answer to "walk me through a penetration test you've done" in any future interview.
