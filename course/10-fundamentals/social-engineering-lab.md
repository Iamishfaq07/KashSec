# Social Engineering Lab

The main lesson covered *why* phishing and social engineering work — this lab builds the actual pattern-recognition skill through repeated, deliberate practice, plus a taste of the defensive side: teaching others to spot what you've learned to spot.

## Safe practice exercises

1. **Analyze 5 real phishing emails.** Use your own spam/junk folder (a genuinely rich, real-world source), or [Google's Phishing Quiz](https://phishingquiz.withgoogle.com/) if you'd prefer curated examples. For each one, identify at minimum: the sender's actual email address (not just the display name), any urgency or fear language used, and where any links actually point (hover, don't click).
2. **Identify red flags systematically.** For each of the 5 emails, write out every red flag you can find — generic greetings, spelling/grammar inconsistencies, mismatched sender domains, suspicious attachments, requests for credentials or payment. Building a checklist habit here is more valuable than any single email you analyze.
3. **Role-play a vishing (voice phishing) scenario.** With a willing friend or family member, take turns: one person plays a caller pretending to be "IT support" or "your bank's fraud department," using real social engineering tactics (urgency, authority, fear) — the other practices staying calm, refusing to give any information, and verifying through a separate channel instead. This is genuinely one of the fastest ways to internalize resistance to these tactics, because experiencing the pressure (even in a harmless role-play) is different from just reading about it.
4. **Create an awareness poster.** Design a simple one-page poster (even hand-drawn or made in any basic tool) illustrating one specific scam pattern and how to spot it — e.g., a mockup of a fake login page next to the real one, with the differences circled. Teaching a concept visually is one of the best ways to confirm you actually understand it.

5. **Audit your own OSINT footprint.** Search your own name, and separately your work/school email address, across a general search engine and social media. Note anything a stranger could learn in five minutes that could plausibly be used to craft a convincing pretext against you specifically — your employer, your manager's name, a recent trip, a hobby group you belong to. This is the exact reconnaissance step a real spear-phisher performs before writing a targeted message, and seeing your own footprint through that lens is genuinely eye-opening the first time.

## Key lessons from this exercise

- **People are consistently the weakest link** — not because people are careless, but because social engineering is specifically designed to exploit normal, reasonable human trust and time pressure, not stupidity.
- **Always verify before acting**, through a channel you initiated yourself, never one provided by the suspicious message.
- **Small details give away scams** — a slightly wrong sender domain, unusual phrasing, a link that doesn't quite match — which is exactly why the systematic red-flag checklist from Task 2 matters more than gut instinct alone.

:::warn Never actually deploy social engineering against real people without authorization
Everything in this lab is either analysis of existing (already-sent) phishing content, or consensual role-play with a willing participant. Actually crafting and sending a phishing email to real, non-consenting people — even "as a test," even against coworkers "for fun" — is both unethical and, in many jurisdictions, illegal without extremely specific, documented authorization (this is what professional social engineering assessments require, covered more in Path 14).
:::

:::challenge Write a 1-page awareness guide
Using everything from this lab, write a short, plain-language guide for non-technical friends or family — the audience most vulnerable to these attacks. Cover: what phishing looks like, the "verify through a separate channel" rule, and what to do if they suspect they've already clicked something. This is a genuinely useful real-world deliverable, not just a course exercise.
:::

## Key takeaway

Recognizing social engineering is a trainable pattern-matching skill, built through repeated exposure to real examples and explicit red-flag checklists — exactly like any other security skill in this course, it improves dramatically with deliberate practice rather than passive awareness alone.
